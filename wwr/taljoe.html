Joint evaluation

Why the six are worth more together

Circularity

Each division is the others' largest customer

Helios powers Cortex's data centres at internal cost. Cortex's models run Kinetic's robots. Kinetic builds Orbit's rockets and Helios's plants. Orbit lifts Vitae's zero-gravity protein work. Pesa settles every internal transfer. Roughly 21% of group revenue is intercompany — eliminated on consolidation, but it means six businesses that would each pay a market margin instead pay cost.

Capital

One balance sheet funds six capital cycles

Pesa and Cortex throw off cash on short cycles. Helios, Orbit and Vitae consume it on ten- to twenty-year cycles. Independently, the long-cycle three would be permanently at the mercy of the credit market. Inside the group they are funded from operations, which is the single largest reason they moved faster than every listed competitor.

Correlation

The risks are genuinely different

A fusion regulatory setback, an AI compute glut, a clinical failure and a payments rule change are not correlated events. Group cash flow volatility over the last six years is roughly a third of the weighted average volatility of the six divisions taken alone — which is what allows TALJOE to hold an investment-grade rating while carrying frontier risk.

Sequencing

The order of founding was the strategy

Money first, because it produced float. Then energy, because energy is the input to everything. Then launch, then intelligence, then labour, then medicine. Every division was started only once the one before it could pay for it. Nothing in the group was built on outside capital until 2021.

What it is

The business

Cortex started with a boring observation: Helios was generating more power at night than anyone in East Africa wanted to buy, and the cheapest thing in the world to move to cheap power is computation. The first Cortex data centre was built beside a hydro plant in Jinja in 2016 for one reason — the electricity there cost a fifth of what it cost in Frankfurt.

Ten years later that arbitrage is the entire company. Cortex owns 2.1 gigawatts of operating compute across nineteen campuses, and it owns the power feeding almost all of it. Competitors buy compute and rent power. Cortex builds both and pays internal cost for the expensive half.

On top of that base sit three layers the group sells outward: the Kalíba family of foundation models, the Sunbird inference processors designed in-house and fabricated under a joint venture in Taiwan and Arizona, and Cortex Agents — the enterprise product that does the work rather than assisting with it.

Pricing is deliberately unusual. Agents are billed per completed task against a service-level guarantee, not per token or per user. That single decision moved Cortex out of the software procurement budget and into the operations budget, which is roughly forty times larger.

Why this can carry a trillion on its own

Global spending on white-collar labour is somewhere near $28 trillion a year. Cortex does not need to replace it — it needs to take a small percentage of the portion that is routine, verifiable and language-bound. At 18% of realised savings on 6% of that labour pool, the revenue line alone clears $300B without a single new product. Everything above that is upside from silicon and grid.

The defensibility is not the model. Models converge. The defensibility is that Cortex owns its electricity, designs its own chips, and sells against an outcome rather than a subscription — three moats that a well-funded competitor cannot cross quickly even with an equally good model.

Principal risk

Capital intensity and concentration. Cortex's capital programme runs at roughly $96B a year, and a two-year demand pause would strand a meaningful share of it. Regulatory exposure is also asymmetric: the outcome-pricing model attracts labour-law scrutiny in twelve jurisdictions.

What it is

The business

Helios is the oldest industrial business in the group and, by revenue, the largest. It began in 2011 with fourteen solar micro-grids built to power TALJOE's own WiFi masts, because the diesel bill was larger than the internet revenue. The villages around the masts asked to buy the surplus. That surplus is now 418 gigawatts of firm capacity across 61 countries.

The strategy has never changed: get to the lowest cost per firm megawatt-hour, then sell it long. Helios does not chase retail customers. It signs twenty-year contracts with the entities that cannot function without guaranteed power — smelters, data centres, desalination plants, fertiliser producers, railways and national grids.

Three technologies do the work. Sunfield perovskite-tandem manufacturing, at 2.1 TW of annual module output, produces panels at roughly a third of the 2020 cost floor. Kigezi iron-air storage — cheap, heavy, non-flammable, unglamorous — turns intermittent generation into firm capacity for about $18 per kilowatt-hour of storage. And since 2024, Helios Fusion, with fourteen operating plants supplying baseload that does not depend on weather at all.

Fusion gets the headlines. Iron-air made the money.

Why this can carry a trillion on its own

Electricity is the only market on earth that is simultaneously enormous, growing, and impossible to substitute. Global spend is roughly $6 trillion a year and demand is rising because everything that used to burn something now plugs in — vehicles, heat, steel, fertiliser and above all computation. A single-digit share of that market, contracted for twenty years, is a trillion-dollar business without any technological heroics.

The multiple is lower than the group average on purpose. Utility cash flows are worth less per dollar than software cash flows. What Helios provides the group is not a high multiple — it is certainty, and the input cost that makes Cortex and Kinetic competitive.

Principal risk

Political and regulatory. Helios Direct removes industrial load from national grids, which shifts fixed grid costs onto residential ratepayers. Four governments have already legislated against it. Fusion also carries a concentrated technical risk: a serious fault at one plant would likely halt the fleet.

What it is

The business

Kinetic exists because Helios needed to build solar factories faster than it could hire and train people to run them. The first Nkuba units were built for internal use in 2019 and were, by any honest measure, bad. The fifth generation is not.

The commercial decision that made Kinetic a trillion-dollar business was refusing to sell the hardware. A Nkuba unit is never bought; it is contracted at an hourly rate against a defined task, with Kinetic retaining ownership, maintenance, insurance, retraining and liability. Customers compare it to a wage, not to a capital purchase — and that comparison is the whole sales process.

The fleet is 4.1 million units across warehousing, agriculture, construction, mining, cleaning, port handling and electronics assembly. Every unit reports to Kinetic OS, which means a manoeuvre learned by one machine in Rotterdam is available to every machine on the network within hours. That compounding is the reason the gap to the second-place manufacturer widened rather than narrowed after 2023.

Alongside the humanoid fleet, Kinetic Freight operates 610,000 autonomous trucks and 240 port terminals — lower margin, but it is the data source that taught the rest of the fleet to handle the unpredictable outdoors.

Why this can carry a trillion on its own

Manual and semi-skilled labour is roughly a $16 trillion annual global cost line. Kinetic charges materially less than the wage it displaces and still holds a 34% margin, because a leased machine works three shifts, does not travel, and improves every week. What it is

The business

Orbit was bought, not founded. In 2014 TALJOE paid $41 million for the wreckage of a European launch company — three engines, a test stand and about ninety engineers who had nowhere else to go. Uganda's position almost exactly on the equator turned out to be worth more than the assets: the group's primary launch site gives roughly 5% more payload to equatorial orbit than any northern spaceport.

The Nsenene heavy-lift vehicle is fully reusable and flies, on average, every eleven hours across four sites. Marginal cost to low Earth orbit is $41 per kilogram. At that price, the constraint on space is no longer transport — it is imagination, which is why the interesting half of Orbit is no longer the rockets.

Orbital Foundry manufactures things that are impossible to make in gravity: perfect semiconductor crystals, defect-free fluoride optical fibre, and organ scaffolds grown without a support structure. Vacuum is free up there, and so is a temperature gradient of three hundred degrees. Foundry is 22% of Orbit's revenue and 41% of its profit.

Halo, the 41,000-satellite direct-to-device constellation, is the volume business — it connects handsets with no ground infrastructure at all, which is why 61% of its subscribers are in Africa and South Asia. And a small, patient team is prospecting metals in near-Earth asteroids, on a twenty-year clock.

What it is

The business

Vitae was spun out of Cortex in 2020, when the protein-structure work being done as a research curiosity started producing candidate molecules faster than anyone in the group could evaluate them. Joel Talemwa's decision to fund it as a full division rather than license the output is, financially, the most contested call he has made — and personally the least ambiguous. His father died of a treatable cardiac condition in 2007 because the nearest diagnostic machine was in Kampala.

The scientific position is narrow and specific. Vitae does not treat diseases one at a time. It targets the small number of cellular processes that produce most age-related disease — epigenetic drift, senescent cell accumulation, mitochondrial decline — on the argument that fixing an upstream cause is cheaper than managing forty downstream symptoms.

Thirty-one therapies are approved in at least one major jurisdiction. The flagship partial-reprogramming programme has, in its largest published cohort, produced a measurable reversal in biological age markers that has held for four years. It is not immortality and Vitae's own literature says so on the first page. It is roughly a decade of additional healthy function, which is enough to reorganise the economics of every pension system on earth.

The company's other half is far duller and pays for the first: Vitae Atlas, a subscription diagnostic that reads eleven ageing biomarkers quarterly, and a generative protein design service licensed to most of the pharmaceutical industry.

Why this can carry a trillion on its own

Global healthcare spending is about $12 trillion a year, and the large majority of it is spent on people over sixty managing conditions caused by the same handful of biological processes. A therapy that delays those processes does not compete with a drug — it competes with a decade of hospital admissions. Health systems will pay a great deal for that, and they will pay on outcome, which is exactly how Vitae prices.

There is also a moat that is uncomfortable to state plainly: the four-year outcome data cannot be replicated quickly by anyone, because replicating it requires four years.

What it is

The business

Pesa is where TALJOE started: one mobile-money agent booth in Nyendo, Masaka, in 2009, taking a commission on deposits and withdrawals for people the banking system had never intended to serve. Everything else in this group was paid for, in the beginning, out of that float.

The insight that scaled it was not technological. It was that the expensive part of moving money is not the transfer — it is knowing who the other party is. Pesa built Pesa ID, a portable identity and reputation layer, and then built the payment rails on top of it, rather than the other way round. That ordering is why Pesa's fraud and default losses are a fraction of the industry's.

Today Pesa Rails settles $71 trillion a year — cross-border trade, salaries, remittances, government disbursements and machine-to-machine payments between the group's own robots and grid assets. The take rate is deliberately tiny, between 4 and 19 basis points depending on corridor, because a small rate on a very large number is more defensible than a large rate that invites regulation and competition.

Pesa Credit underwrites against the identity graph rather than a credit bureau, which is how it lends profitably to 480 million people who have no formal credit history at all.

Biography

From Nyendo Road to a $16 trillion balance sheet

Joel Talemwa was born in Masaka in 1986, the third of five children. His mother taught at a primary school; his father repaired radios and small engines out of a workshop attached to the family house, and it was there, taking apart transistor sets he was supposed to be cleaning, that Talemwa learned electronics.

He read electrical engineering at Makerere University but left in his third year, in 2009, after a lecturer explained that the national grid could not economically reach the villages he had grown up between. He has said since that he did not leave in protest — he left because he had already worked out what he wanted to build and could not see how another eighteen months of lectures would help.

That year he registered Talemwa Joel Enterprises — TALJOE — in Nyendo, Masaka, with UGX 2.4 million borrowed from an uncle and a savings group. The business was a mobile-money agency and three WiFi masts along the Masaka–Mbarara highway. It was profitable in its fourth month.

The diesel generators that powered those masts cost more than the internet revenue they enabled, so in 2011 he bought solar panels. Neighbours asked to buy the surplus power. That is Helios. In 2016 he noticed Helios was wasting electricity at night, and the cheapest thing to move to cheap power is computation. That is Cortex. Each division in this group came from a problem in the previous one, and he has never started a business to enter a market.

Talemwa was 30 when the group first passed a billion dollars in valuation, 37 when it passed a trillion, and 40 today. He has taken no outside equity in five of the six divisions, and the group's public float remains 5.4%.

He is not, by the standards of people at his level of wealth, a public figure. He gives roughly two interviews a year, does not sit on outside boards, and has declined every honorary position offered to him by a government. He still lives in Masaka, works from the group's Kampala office four days a week, and keeps the original Nyendo booth — now a small museum with the first ledger book in a case.

The Talemwa Foundation holds 11.4% of the group, funding rural diagnostic clinics across East Africa and a scholarship scheme that has put 214,000 students through technical and university education. He has committed the entire holding, and the majority of his personal stake, to be transferred to the Foundation on his death — a decision he has said publicly is not generosity but arithmetic, because no family needs ten trillion dollars and every one of them would be ruined by it.

He was married in 2012 and has three children, none of whom hold shares or executive positions. Group policy, written by him in 2023, bars any descendant from an executive role.

Method

Four rules he actually runs the company by

Rule one

Own the input

Never build a business whose main cost is bought from someone with pricing power. If the input matters, buy the company that makes it or build one.

Rule two

Sell the outcome

Charge for the work done, not for access to the tool. It moves you into a bigger budget and it aligns you with the customer.

Rule three

Fund it from the last one

Every division was paid for by the division before it. Outside money buys speed and sells control, and control is the only thing that cannot be bought back.

Rule four

Stay where you started

The head office is in Kampala because the hardest problems the group solves are visible from there. Distance from the customer is the most expensive thing on a balance sheet.
